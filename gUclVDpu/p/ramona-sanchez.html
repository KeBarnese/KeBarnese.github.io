Occupation
Owned and ran a restaurant in Reno called "Ramona's Kitchen," which opened around 10 Aug 1947 inside "Roaring Camp," a Western-curiosity tourist attraction at Second and Lake Streets — merchants' lunch 80–90 cents, "the finest genuine Mexican food," steak and chicken dinners, "Ramona Sanchez at the range" — then, by May 1950, at its own new location, 217 Lake St. (a move the reopening ad calls "Ramona's Kitchen is Back," implying a gap between the two), where it stayed through at least 1956 (surviving at least one more brief closure and reopening, in Aug 1953, "under same management"), and in her last years relocated again to Virginia City — earlier, on son James's 1922 birth certificate, her occupation is given as "bookkeeper & stenographer," at age 24
Court record
Money trouble at the restaurant went back further than the 1952 accident: in May 1949 the state labor commissioner, R. N. Gibson, sued her on behalf of a former employee, Emilio Garcia, for $382.40 in unpaid wages, plus $270 in penalties and attorney fees. The complaint said she had employed Garcia from January to March 1949 at $8 a day plus room and board, and never paid him. This article also places "Ramona's Kitchen" inside the Roaring Camp attraction on Lake St., confirming it as the same venue that opened in Aug 1947 (see the opening ad, below) No outcome of this suit has surfaced either. Taken with the later 1952-1956 trouble below, it suggests running "Ramona's Kitchen" was a genuinely difficult, marginal business for much of its Reno life, not a stable success interrupted only by the Harold's Club accident

A smaller item three days later, same year: on 22 May 1949 she reported her purse — $95 cash and a $6 check — stolen off the restaurant counter sometime between midnight and 1:05 a.m., confirming "Ramona's Kitchen" kept late hours, into the small hours of the morning A minor theft report, included mainly for the operating-hours detail and as another small data point in a hard year for the business

Money was still tight two years later, just months before the Harold's Club fall: in Mar 1951 she was sued for $348.50 in unpaid advertising bills, for ads that had run in the Guest Informant, a trade paper published by Pacific Hotel Publications, Inc.; the debt had been assigned to a collection agent, D. P. Gibson, who brought the suit and sought a further $150 in attorney fees This puts a financial-trouble filing roughly five months before the Aug 1951 Harold's Club accident, on top of the 1949 wage suit — a pattern of struggle bracketing the accident on both sides, not one triggered by it

In May 1952 she sued Harold's Club, one of Reno's biggest casinos, for $34,103.48 over a fall on its motor stairway the previous August. Her complaint alleged negligent, careless maintenance of the stairs; injuries listed included a concussion with a long period of unconsciousness, shock to her nervous system, a sprained left knee, a fractured left ankle, and traumatic arthritis in the leg, some of it permanent. The damages she sought were itemized in detail: $860.15 in medical costs, $2,412 for the wages of a cook she had to hire to run "Ramona's Kitchen" in her place for 201 days, $5,831.33 in profits she said the restaurant lost while she couldn't manage it herself, and $25,000 for physical and mental pain and permanent injury. Attorney William L. Hammersmith represented her The case went to trial that October, before District Judge Harold O. Taber, without a jury; the escalator itself (called a "motor stairway" in the original filing) was the subject at issue, and Ramona testified the handrail wasn't coordinated with the steps for speed, causing her fall. The defense, for its part, alleged she had been intoxicated at the time — a claim she disputed — and argued the handrail and steps were properly timed. Only Ramona and her physician testified for her side; the casino called more than a dozen witnesses. She won at trial: Judge Taber awarded her $13,000 (well short of the $34,000 she'd sought), ruling that a gambling club running five bars had a duty to protect intoxicated and sober patrons alike, even to the point of physically stopping them from entering the escalator. Harold's Club appealed, and on 21 Oct 1954 the Nevada Supreme Court reversed the award outright: Associate Justice Milton B. Badt, joined by Chief Justice Edgar Eather and Associate Justice Charles Merrill, held there was no precedent for imposing so high a duty of care, and that the club's attendants had repeatedly warned her not to enter the escalator, warnings the opinion says she'd ignored. She ended the case with nothing The reversal opinion's language — describing repeated warnings not to enter, which went ignored — reads as the court effectively accepting the defense's 1952 intoxication claim as the operative fact, even though Ramona had disputed it at trial and the wire report never uses the word directly. It's a striking case regardless of outcome: proof of a woman running her own restaurant business seriously enough to sue a major casino over lost profits and a hired replacement cook, see it through a two-day trial against a stack of defense witnesses, win at the district court, and lose it all on appeal two years later

Money stayed tight afterward: in Jul 1953, just over a year later, her landlords Anson and W. C. Van Buren sued her for $1,400 in back rent on the store and basement space she leased at 217 Lake St. — the address of "Ramona's Kitchen" itself — claiming she was seven months behind (Jan. 1 to Aug. 1, 1953) and had refused either to pay or vacate after written notice. They also sought $475 in attorneys' fees No outcome found for this suit either. It follows the Harold's Club fall by about a year, though given the 1949 wage suit above, financial strain at the restaurant wasn't new by this point — the accident may have made an existing problem worse rather than caused it outright. Notably, a reopening ad for "Ramona's Kitchen" ran on 7 Aug 1953 — nine days after this suit was filed and about a month before the September judgment — announcing it "Now Open 24 Hours ... Under Same Management," which suggests the restaurant closed briefly and reopened at the same 217 Lake St. address right in the middle of this run of financial trouble

Barely a month later, in Sep 1953, she confessed judgment to a separate $1,780 suit brought by F. W. Forwood back in March — admitting she owed $1,500 on a promissory note secured by a chattel mortgage on the restaurant's own equipment and furnishings at 217 Lake St., and agreeing to a sale of that property to satisfy the debt, plus $250 in attorney fees and $30 in court costs Three money troubles inside about sixteen months — the injury suit, the back-rent suit, and now a debt secured against her restaurant's own property, settled by agreeing to sell it off — read together as a real financial decline for "Ramona's Kitchen" in its Lake St. years, not just one bad break

It got worse before it got better: in Apr 1956 the federal government itself seized the restaurant's equipment — steam table, counter, stools, steel sink and station, sign, hoods, water heater, tables, chairs, gas range, and dishwasher, everything at 217 Lake St. — for nonpayment of delinquent internal revenue taxes owed by "Ramona G. Sanchez dba Ramona's Kitchen," and scheduled it all for public auction on 9 Apr 1956 at the Reno post office building Whatever happened at that auction, it wasn't the end of the business: her 1966 obituary describes her still running "Ramona's Kitchen" a decade later, by then relocated to Virginia City — so she evidently rebuilt or reopened after this, one way or another, though how isn't recorded here

Family recollection (not documented in any of the above) holds that a gambling habit was the actual source of this long run of financial trouble — a plausible explanation the newspaper record doesn't rule out, given that her original injury happened inside a casino and the casino's own defense alleged she'd been drinking there at the time. None of it touches her cooking, which every source that mentions the restaurant, without exception, treats as genuinely excellent.

Their story

Crossed from Mazatlan as a girl of about thirteen, married a Spanish fisherman's son, raised five children through a divorce, and ended up running her own restaurant in Reno. She was the one who ended the marriage in practice: a Pomona newspaper item from November 1933 names her, not Roman, as the complaining witness in a non-support case, already living apart from him in Pomona while he was "of Los Angeles." Whatever the outcome of that case, it shows the break happening years earlier than the family seems to have remembered it — where she lived her last twenty years. Her papers recorded her height at six feet: a formidable woman by any measure. She made her name in Reno running a restaurant called "Ramona's Kitchen," which she opened around 10 Aug 1947 inside "Roaring Camp" — a Reno tourist attraction at Second and Lake Streets billing itself as "A Western World's Fair," with Old West guns and music boxes on display and a main draw named "Frisco Pearl, the most beautiful woman in the world" — before the restaurant moved, by May 1950, to a new address of its own — 217 Lake St., which its reopening ad announced as "Ramona's Kitchen is Back," hinting at a gap in between. It stayed at 217 Lake St. through at least the mid-1950s, weathering at least one more short closure and reopening in Aug 1953, before, in her last years, moving again to Virginia City, where she died. Running it was a struggle for much of its life: as early as 1949 the state labor commissioner sued her for unpaid wages owed to a cook, and in early 1951, just months before her better-documented accident that August, she was sued again over an unpaid advertising bill — money trouble on both sides of the Harold's Club fall, not just after it. Family recollection holds that a gambling habit was the real cause behind the money trouble, not a shortfall in the business itself or her cooking — and the paper trail doesn't contradict that: her injury happened inside a casino in the first place, its defense alleged she'd been drinking there, and none of the financial or legal record ever questions her food. Whatever pulled at her finances, her cooking itself was never in doubt — obituaries, ads, and articles alike consistently single it out as the real draw of "Ramona's Kitchen." She ran it seriously enough as a business to sue over it: in 1952, after a bad fall on an escalator at Harold's Club left her with a concussion, a fractured ankle, and lasting arthritis in her leg, she sought over $34,000 in damages — not just for her injuries, but for the wages of the cook she'd had to hire and the profits her restaurant lost while she recovered. It went to a two-day bench trial that October, where the casino's attorneys countered with an allegation that she'd been drinking, which she denied, and outnumbered her two witnesses with more than a dozen of their own — and she won, at $13,000. It didn't last: two years later, in Oct 1954, the Nevada Supreme Court reversed the award entirely, ruling the club had no such high duty of care and that she'd ignored the attendants' warnings not to enter the escalator. She came away with nothing. Money stayed tight after that: by the summer of 1953 her landlords were suing her for $1,400 in back rent on the restaurant's own address, 217 Lake St., claiming seven months unpaid and that she'd refused to pay or leave, and within weeks she was confessing judgment on a separate $1,500 debt secured against the restaurant's own equipment, agreeing to sell it off to settle the claim. By 1956 it had gone further still: the federal government seized the entire kitchen — steam table, sink, range, dishwasher, and all — for unpaid taxes and put it up for public auction. Whatever happened at that sale, the business outlived it: her 1966 obituary still has her running "Ramona's Kitchen," by then moved to Virginia City. Her obituary listed sons James (Los Angeles), John (San Francisco), and Roman (with the Merchant Marine), and daughters Celia Moralez (San Francisco) and Grace Bell (Los Angeles).
