A far lighter item: in Apr 1937 the Saginaw News ran a filler item, "Target Practice," about a stray .22 rifle round from the basement of "Frank Barnese, 936 North Sixth avenue," that put a hole in a passing motorist's car window. No one was hurt, no charge appears to have followed, and the paper treated it as a joke rather than news 936 North Sixth was Frank's own address at the time — 20 years old and living on his own, ahead of his 1944 marriage, a few doors down from the family's 1000/1006 North Sixth store

A real loss came later: in Apr 1946 — two years after his marriage, presumably back in Saginaw between his 1940 enlistment and the eventual move west — a "Frank Barnese, of 1311 Kirk" reported his home broken into through an unlocked window while he and his wife were out Monday night, the thief leaving with a bedspread, a fur coat, a man's topcoat, a woman's topcoat, women's underclothing and stockings, a suitcase, a makeup kit, insurance papers, and a child's glass bank with $10 in it — $280 in all 1311 Kirk is not independently confirmed as this Frank's address elsewhere, but it's a short walk from the family's Sixth-and-Kirk store, and the mix of stolen goods (a man's and a woman's coat, women's clothing) fits a married couple's household two years after his 1944 wedding — presented as a likely but not certain match

Their story

He enlisted on 4 November 1940 in Detroit — Regular Army, assigned to the Hawaiian Department — which is how a Saginaw boy came to be at Hickam Field when Pearl Harbor was bombed. He served on as Air Force ground crew through the Guadalcanal campaign, seeing the war's first and worst days. The records bear out the family story: his wartime hospital admissions include malaria in the spring of 1944 and dengue fever that December — Pacific tropical diseases, the malaria the one that would flare for years afterward, treated with quinine. On an island where more men fell to malaria than to bullets, it was the part of the war his body wouldn't let him leave. He never spoke of the rest.

Civilian life had its lighter chapters too. On 6 May 1955 he opened the Pico-Rivera Bowl at 5054 South San Gabriel Place — twenty deluxe Brunswick lanes, billed as the first on the West Coast with the new Brunswick Crown Imperial line, plus a billiard room and coffee shop, with free prizes and summer leagues to draw a crowd for the grand opening. He was still running it eight years later when, in November 1963, he made the local paper trying to legalize a "Card Palace" in the city — a sixteen-page proposed amendment to the city code, drawn up with his brother Anthony and a partner, Dominic Distaso, after the trio was denied a license for a gambling card room under the city's existing laws. Their cover letter predicted card rooms would bring the city $100,000 a year in revenue; it didn't move the council, which rejected the amendment unanimously, with Councilwoman Ruth Benell leading the opposition and enough of a crowd in the chamber that she asked to have the whole thing read into the record just to settle it quickly. The trio didn't even attend that meeting — Frank's own submission had left off a return address, so the city manager had no way to notify him when to show up. Frank, who with Anthony and Distaso had already tried and failed to get the same law passed in Walnut City, said the council had "railroaded" the decision through: "We had no voice in the matter... we don't know what we're going to do." By month's end they were weighing an initiative petition to put it to a public vote instead, and Frank told the Daily News he liked their odds, citing the narrow defeat of a county-wide anti-poker measure the year before: "According to the last vote, I think it might pass." It didn't. By 4 December 1963 Frank told the Daily News the whole thing was dead: "We presented the proposed amendment in good faith... and we were taken completely by surprise by the strong opposition which followed our presentation. However, that's all water under the bridge now, and there is no need to discuss the matter any further. We have dropped it and as far as I'm concerned, it is now a dead issue." Pico Rivera never got its card room.

He got there in the end, just somewhere else. Five years after Pico Rivera turned him down, the Cabazon City Council voted 3–1 on 28 May 1968 to grant a gambling license to Frank and a new partner, Edward Roban, a retired Los Angeles County fireman from Covina. The two put up $25,000 for the license — two cashier's checks, one for $15,000 and one for $10,000 — plus a $50 application fee and a $100 permit fee, and the front-page story in the Banning paper described the applause after approval as "thunderous." It made Cabazon's Desert Sands Casino, until then the only gambling house in town, face its first real competition; Frank and Roban were reportedly planning to open in the former Desert Sands building itself, at Main and Broadway, and had the right to do so "this minute." The paper still identified him by the bowling alley — "Barnese, of Whittier, owns a bowling alley in Pico Riviera" — a nice throughline from the 1963 defeat to this 1968 win.
