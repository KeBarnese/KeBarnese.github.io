Barnese · Barnesi (Sicily)

Salvatore (Sam) Barnesi

1888 – 1971
Great-grandfather
Older generations — His actual Pachino birth act (No. 232, 28 April 1888) now settles both questions. It confirms his parents outright — Giuseppe Barnisi, aged ~50, and Carmela — vindicating the earlier guess from the Sicilian naming custom, since his first son was indeed named Joseph. (The mother's maiden surname reads Barnizzi — the form the Pachino registrar himself used in 1908; the Lascari clerk, taking it down at second hand 250 km away, wrote Barnizri.) And it resolves the birthplace: he really was born in Pachino, in the far southeast of Sicily (province of Syracuse), some 250 km from the Lascari/Palermo towns of the Covelli and Muffoletto lines. How a Pachino man came to marry into and settle with a Lascari family is now the standing puzzle — but the birthplace itself is no longer in doubt.

Court record
Shot and killed Tom Smith, a 17-year-old, in the early hours of 23 Dec 1930 outside his store, after — by the account Salvatore gave police — a brick was thrown through the front window and Smith began climbing through it; the fourth break-in attempt on the store in recent months, he said, and the reason he had been sleeping there himself. He fired a shotgun, emptying it as Smith climbed through the broken window. The Saginaw News reported it on its front page as a store owner shooting an intruder in self-defense, and named Smith by race in the flat language of 1930 newspaper reporting; an AP wire version, picked up the same day by the Bay City Times, repeats the same account but does not yet name Smith, calling him only an "unidentified Negro" — evidently an earlier edition, before identification No inquest finding, coroner's verdict, or follow-up coverage has turned up — only these same-day accounts, both ultimately from the same police version of events. Whether the shooting was investigated further, contested by Smith's family, or simply accepted is not something these articles can settle, and the era's police and press did not always scrutinize a white shooter's account of self-defense against a Black victim with the same rigor they might have applied otherwise. This record is presented as what it is: a single, one-sided contemporary account (run twice, in two papers) of a young man's death, not a verdict

A separate incident, nine years later: shortly after 9 p.m. on 19 Nov 1938, two young gunmen held up his store at 1000 North Sixth, forcing him at gunpoint to hand over $250 from the register. According to the Saginaw News's report the very next day, as the pair turned to leave Salvatore pulled his own gun from his pocket and fired — but, in the paper's words, "his aim was bad," and none of his bullets took effect. The robbers fired back; their aim was bad too, and the exchange did no more than minor damage to the store and stock. No one, by this same-week account, was hit. Within days Salvatore identified one of the two, 21-year-old Detroiter Frank Perry, obtained a warrant accusing him of armed robbery — then decided against pursuing prosecution. Perry stayed in the Saginaw county jail regardless, serving out an unrelated 10-day sentence for reckless driving; it was that arrest, and a license-plate tip from two deer hunters who said they'd been robbed by men in Perry's wrecked car, that had linked him to the grocery holdup in the first place. This was the opening job of a young "Midland bandit gang" that idolized John Dillinger and went on to stage eight robberies in Saginaw and one in Bay City over a two-month spree netting roughly $1,000, before mass arrests broke it up in Jan 1939 — at which point a second suspect, 17-year-old Roy Spann, confessed to all eight jobs and told police that Salvatore's shot at this first one had in fact wounded him in the leg These two contemporary accounts genuinely disagree on whether Salvatore's shot connected: the Saginaw News's day-after report (20 Nov 1938) says his aim was bad and no bullets took effect, while its own retrospective two months later (26 Jan 1939), built on Spann's confession, says he was wounded in the leg by that same shot. Both are presented here rather than silently reconciled — it's possible the first article simply reflected what was visible/known that night (the robbers fled and no one collapsed at the scene) while Spann's later, private admission to police revealed an injury that hadn't been apparent, or that one account is simply wrong; nothing settles it either way. Reported in the Saginaw News, 20 Nov 1938 (the robbery itself), 29 Nov 1938 (Perry's identification and the declined prosecution), and 26 Jan 1939 (Spann's confession, five other arrests in the same gang). No indication Salvatore faced any legal consequence for firing at the fleeing robbers, consistent with the era's treatment of an armed-robbery victim defending his store; his reasons for declining to prosecute Perry aren't stated in any article — possibly a practical business owner's calculation about the cost and risk of pursuing a case further, though that's inference, not something the record states

A far lower-stakes brush with the law came later that same year: in Dec 1939 he was one of 20 Saginaw-area liquor dealers hauled before the state liquor control board on various alleged license abuses. His charges were possessing gambling equipment, extending credit, and permitting insanitary conditions. His defense on the gambling count has survived verbatim — the "gambling equipment" was a penny gum machine on which, he told the commissioner, "the customer can sometimes get cigarets if he wants them"; the credit accusation he chalked up to investigators misreading his books No outcome for his case was reported in this article; it's presented here as a small, mildly comic footnote to the Sixth Street store's history, not as an established violation

Their story

The Barnesi patriarch who left Lascari for America. He sailed alone, arriving 11 August 1910, when his daughter Mary was not yet a year old — he had been there for her birth the previous September, but would miss her toddler years. Carmela and the little girl did not cross to join him until July 1912, almost two years later, by which time she was nearly three. He left an infant and was reunited with a child. The family name moved through four spellings, each of them on a real document: Barnisi (the Sicilian original, on his 1908 marriage act), Barnesi (his ship manifest and earliest American records), the clipped Barnes (on his early U.S. census records), and finally Barnese. None of that was the family deciding to respell itself. Salvatore's father was illiterate — his 1888 birth act notes he could not sign — so for most of these lives the name was written FOR them, by whichever clerk held the pen, who set down what he heard. In that world a surname was a sound, not a fixed string of letters. You can watch it happen in miniature in Salvatore's own mother's maiden name, recorded Barnizri on one act and Barnirzi on another, the r and z simply swapped; and in his birthplace, rendered 'Cesille' by a Michigan draft clerk who had never heard of Pachino. A single, stable spelling only arrived once the American-born generation could write the name for themselves. He made a life on this side of the ocean: he founded a grocery store at Sixth and Kirk in Saginaw in the late 1920s. Two years in, in the early hours of 23 December 1930, he shot and killed a 17-year-old, Tom Smith, outside the store — by Salvatore's own account to police, the fourth break-in attempt on the store in recent months, which was why he had taken to sleeping there; he said Smith threw a brick through the front window and was climbing in when he emptied a shotgun at him. The Saginaw News ran it on the front page as a straightforward case of a store owner defending his property against a burglar, in the flat, racially-marked language typical of 1930 newspaper reporting; an AP wire version, printed the same day in the Bay City Times before Smith had been named, tells the identical story. No inquest result, coroner's verdict, or later coverage has surfaced to say anything more about how the death was investigated, or whether it was contested — these are same-day accounts of a young man's death, both from the same police version of events, not the full record of it, and they're presented here as exactly that. A second, unrelated brush with gunfire came nine years later: shortly after 9 p.m. on 19 November 1938, two young gunmen forced $250 from his register at gunpoint, and as they turned to leave he drew his own gun and fired — the next day's paper says his aim was bad and no one was hit, though the robbers' return fire did minor damage to the store. He soon identified one of them, 21-year-old Frank Perry of Detroit, and got a warrant for his arrest, then chose not to prosecute. This turned out to be the opening job of a young Saginaw robbery gang that idolized John Dillinger; when it was rounded up two months later after an eight-robbery spree, the second man, 17-year-old Roy Spann, confessed and told police Salvatore's shot that night had actually wounded him in the leg — directly at odds with the day-after report that no bullets took effect, a contradiction the record doesn't resolve. By the time his son Frank left for the war in 1940, the store was well established. His own 1942 draft registration puts the store at 1000 N. 6th Street in Saginaw and records his birthplace — phonetically — as 'Cesille, Italy,' an unrecognized spelling almost certainly a clerk's rendering of his Sicilian home. He ran the store for roughly a quarter century before handing it to his son, Sam Jr., on his retirement in 1955. The building itself didn't last much longer: starting in mid-1958 it was torn down in stages — built in two phases so the store could stay open, losing only about a week of business across the whole four-month project — and replaced with a $64,000 concrete-block-and-brick supermarket, completed that November. Sam Jr. reopened it under the family name in January 1959 — by which point a Saginaw newspaper noted simply that 'the elder Barnese, retired, lives in California.' Two weekly ads from just after the reopening survive. The first, six days after the grand opening, thanks customers by name for the turnout — "we were overwhelmed by the tremendous crowds... you were wonderful!" — and lists steaks at 69 cents a pound, apples at 29 cents for four pounds, and a page of door-prize winners. The second, a week later, is back to routine business: club steak at 59 cents, fresh pig's feet at 15 cents, a full line of party beverages, and coupon specials on everything from dog food to Kleenex. Between the two, they're the clearest surviving snapshot of the store his son was now running. He spent his last years in California, dying in Tarzana in 1971.
