Their story

Rose's parents, of Collesano. Alessandro Macaluso crossed to America first, sailing from Naples on the Calabria on 13 January 1902 and landing at New York sixteen days later with thirty dollars in his pocket — three times what most of the men listed around him carried. He was thirty, a labourer, and he was going to a brother already on South Clark Street in Chicago. He left behind a wife and three small children, the youngest of them an infant: Rosa was born within a few months either side of his departure, so he may well have sailed before he ever saw her. It was eight years before the family was whole again. Alessandro was still at that Clark Street address — the card gives 377 South Clark — in 1910, when Giuseppa Luisi, a seamstress who could neither read nor write, followed on the Duca d'Aosta with Dominica, Agostino, and eight-year-old Rosa, to join him. The family put down roots in the Chicago–Gary steel belt; an immigration cross-reference card shows Giuseppa still there in 1949, collecting a certificate of arrival at Hammond, Indiana. Her brother, Giuseppe Luisi, stayed behind in Collesano. Alessandro kept ties to the old country: a 1925 manifest catches him sailing back into Chicago from Naples that August, after a visit to Italy where his son Agostino was then living in Lascari. It's a small world of Madonie towns this whole family came from — Collesano, Campofelice, and Lascari all sit within a few miles of one another on the Palermo coast. Giuseppa outlived Alessandro; her own death notice ran in the Chicago Tribune on 20 Jan 1956, under the Americanized name "Rachel Macaluso, nee Louise" — likely Giuseppa Luisi rendered for an English-speaking readership, her maiden surname Luisi becoming "Louise." It names all three of her children by their married identities: Mamie (Peter) Dolce, Gus, and Rose (Sam) Muffoletto — the last an exact match for Rose Macaluso's own marriage to Sam Muffoletto — and counts 11 grandchildren and 13 great-grandchildren by 1956. She was waked at a chapel on South Sacramento Boulevard, sent off from Presentation Church, and buried at Mount Carmel Cemetery.

Neither of them ever became American. In March 1916 Alessandro petitioned the Circuit Court of Cook County from 1214 West Harrison Street, with two Irish neighbours from Sangamon and Morgan Streets standing as his witnesses, and was refused. The card records only the word "Denied" and the date. Why is still to be found — but the likeliest answer is bureaucratic rather than personal: under the 1906 Act a declaration of intention had to be followed by a petition within seven years, and a lapsed declaration would sink an application on paperwork alone. Whatever the reason, it cost Giuseppa too. Until the Cable Act of September 1922 a wife took citizenship automatically when her husband was naturalized, so his refusal was hers as well; and she was still registering as an alien in 1940, thirty years after she landed. She was evidently still trying at seventy-five, collecting a certificate of arrival at Hammond in 1949. Alessandro, meanwhile, never quite let go of Sicily — the 1925 manifest catches him coming home to Chicago at fifty-four from a visit to his son Agostino at Lascari.
