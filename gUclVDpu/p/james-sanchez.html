Court record
Two armed robberies, five years apart — the second now explained by the first. On the night of 6 May 1950 he robbed a sundries store at 301 W. Pacific Coast Hwy, Wilmington: entered with a .38-caliber revolver, cut the phone wires, forced the owner into a back room, then forced the owner's 12-year-old son to join him when the boy walked in minutes later, and fled with about $100. He and his cousin Manuel Guerrero, 29 — both given as of 327 N. Kern Ave., Los Angeles, the same house where his sister Grace and cousin Manuel are separately documented that year — were pulled over a few blocks away, at Pacific Coast Hwy. and Fries St., by patrolmen O. E. Green and M. E. Smith on nothing more than a hunch that they "looked suspicious"; only while searching them did the officers hear the robbery called in over police radio. A .38-caliber revolver and $100 in cash — the exact amount taken — were found on the pair, described as "the clincher," and the store owner identified Sanchez on sight. Guerrero, accused of driving the getaway car, had the charge against him dismissed for lack of identification. Sanchez pleaded guilty on 12 June 1950, was denied probation, and on 13 July 1950 Superior Judge Joseph M. Maltby sentenced him to five years to life in San Quentin The store owner is named "John Schneider" in the Los Angeles Times and the sentencing article, but "John Snyder" in the Long Beach Press-Telegram's arrest report — probably the same man, with Press-Telegram the outlier. Five years later, on 12 Nov 1955 — presumably not long after parole — he held up the Eagle Thrifty Market, 445 S. Virginia St., Reno: assistant manager Alfred Bouteiller forced to open the safe at closing, then driven south past Carson City before being released; Sanchez was arrested the same night on U.S. 395, pleaded guilty within 42 hours, and Judge Gordon Rice sentenced him to not less than five years in the (presumably Nevada) state prison — a second conviction, on top of San Quentin, which explains why probation was no longer on the table

Their story

Roman and Ramona's second son, and the one who walked the roughest road. After the family came apart he was living in Mexico — his own draft card notes he 'was residing in Mexico until 10-12-42' — before turning up in San Diego at nineteen and registering for the war, his mother Ramona named as next-of-kin. He served in the U.S. Navy: an Aviation Machinist's Mate in the aviation unit of the light cruiser USS Portsmouth (CL-102), tending her scout floatplanes, joining the crew in July 1945 and rising to Seaman First Class. Tall like the rest — six foot one and a half, gray eyes, brown hair, a scar across the bridge of his nose. His first known crime came on the night of 6 May 1950, twenty-seven years old and — this is the detail that nails down that this is our James, not some other man of the same name — living at 327 N. Kern Ave., the same house where his sister Grace was boarding that spring and his aunt Dolores Guerrero held the lease. He walked into a sundries store in Wilmington with a .38-caliber revolver, cut the phone wires, and forced the owner into a back room — and when the owner's twelve-year-old son walked in a few minutes later, forced the boy in there too, before fleeing with about a hundred dollars. He and his cousin Manuel Guerrero, who lived at the same address, were pulled over a few blocks away on nothing more than a patrolman's hunch that they "looked suspicious," before the officers even knew a robbery had happened; while they were being searched at the roadside, the holdup came over the radio, and a .38 and exactly $100 in cash turned up on them — what the Los Angeles Times called "the clincher." Guerrero's charge was dropped for lack of evidence, but Sanchez pleaded guilty, was denied probation, and in July 1950 a superior court judge sent him to San Quentin for five years to life. He was out again, on parole most likely, by November 1955, when he tried to cash an $85 check at the Eagle Thrifty Market in Reno and was turned down. He left angry, hid behind a gas-station phone booth on Sierra St. until the store closed, then followed the assistant manager, Alfred Bouteiller, out to his car, jammed a hand in his coat pocket to simulate a gun, and marched him back inside to open the safe. He had Bouteiller drive him south past Carson City, let him go, then pulled off onto a back road near Minden to count the take — less than the $3,000 he'd expected — and threw the checks, silver, and evidence into a river between there and Gardnerville. Deputies caught him on U.S. 395 the same night with $895 in cash still on him. Within forty-two hours he had waived his preliminary hearing and pleaded guilty a second time; this time, with San Quentin already behind him, Judge Gordon Rice sentenced him to not less than five years in the state prison. Two convictions, two states, five years apart — the hardest chapter in this family's story, and now the best-documented one. But his Navy service held: he was buried with military honors at San Joaquin Valley National Cemetery — the same ground as his brother John, the Marine captain. Two brothers, two very different lives, at rest in the same earth. He died on 7 December 2001 — the sixtieth anniversary, to the day, of the Pearl Harbor attack that had drawn his generation into the war. His family chose an epitaph that reaches past every hard chapter and lands on who he was underneath: 'Seeker of the Truth. Forever in our hearts.'
