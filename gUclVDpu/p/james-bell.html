Their story

A Union County farmer of Civil-War age. The 1860 census puts him in Morganfield at 32, with his first wife, Susan C., and their four children — confirming the long-suspected Susan as his first wife. He later married a second Mary E., the mother of Henry. His war is documented in a thick Confederate file — [likely his, though 'James Bell' is a common name]: he enlisted in August 1862 in Adam Johnson's 10th Kentucky Cavalry, the Partisan Rangers. Within a year it was over — captured at Louisville on 2 September 1863, sent first to Camp Chase, Ohio — a smallpox-ridden Union prison at Columbus — and then, in January 1864, to the brand-new Rock Island Barracks in Illinois, where he was among the first prisoners, arriving into an unfinished camp as smallpox and a thirty-below cold snap killed men by the score. There, claiming to be a conscript and deserter, he signed the Oath of Allegiance to the Union on 4 February 1864 and was released — walking out of one of the deadliest prisons in the North within weeks, an oath that very likely saved his life. That oath is what ties the soldier to your James: it gives his residence as Morganfield and his age as 37, and describes him as five foot eleven, dark complexion, light hair, grey eyes. A tall Union County man who rode for the Confederacy, thought better of it, swore to the Union, and went back to his farm — Kentucky's divided war in one life.
