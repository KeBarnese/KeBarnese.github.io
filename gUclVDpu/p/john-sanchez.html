Their story

The eldest of Roman and Ramona's children. His 1942 draft card is unmistakably his: it names his mother, Ramona G. Sanchez of San Diego, as the person who would always know his address. It also shows he had made his own way — at 21 he was a student at the University of San Francisco, a long climb for a boy whose younger sisters had been placed in an orphanage after their parents' divorce. He was a big man: six foot two and a half, 235 pounds, red hair, brown eyes, a ruddy complexion, and a scar on his right wrist — carrying his mother Ramona's remarkable height into the next generation. From that University of San Francisco classroom he went into the Marine Corps as an officer. A San Francisco News profile caught him on 31 July 1944, freshly commissioned out of three months' officer training at Quantico and about to ship out — "on the threshold of new adventure," the reporter called it. The old 235-pound roving tackle had been reforged into "215 of steel-hardened muscle," twenty-three years old, rusty-haired, hard-jawed. Asked to reflect, Lieutenant Sanchez offered only: "But it's great to be back to the fog" — San Francisco humor from a man about to go to war. The same column named a teammate from his USF squad, Captain Doc Haley, already fighting on Saipan; within a year Sanchez would be on that same island himself. He moved up through Saipan in the spring of 1945, and on 5 June 1945 went ashore at Iheya Shima — a small island off Okinawa that the Marines seized in the final weeks of the Okinawa campaign to build airfields for the planned invasion of Japan. A second lieutenant then, he would rise to Captain — the commission his education had opened the door to. But the most surprising chapter came after the war: a six-foot-plus lineman, he turned pro, playing offensive tackle in the NFL and AAFC from 1947 to 1950 — the Detroit Lions and Chicago Rockets, then the Washington Redskins (1947–49) and New York Giants (1949–50) — and was later inducted into the University of San Francisco Athletics Hall of Fame (1975). He then spent forty-five years as a self-employed building contractor, was married to Nan for thirty-seven years, and raised three sons — Jon, Michael, and Steven — in the East Bay. He died in Walnut Creek in 1992 at seventy-one. His headstone reads simply: John C. Sanchez, Capt., U.S. Marine Corps, World War II.
