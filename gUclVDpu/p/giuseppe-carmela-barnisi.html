Their story

Salvatore's parents. Their son's 1888 Pachino birth act names them both — Giuseppe Barnisi and Carmela (maiden surname read here as Barnizzi, the form the Pachino registrar himself used in 1908; the Lascari clerk, taking it down at second hand 250 km away, wrote Barnizri) — and gives Giuseppe's age as fifty, placing his birth around 1838. He gave his trade as industriante, and could not sign his name: the act notes he was illiterate. The family lived in Pachino, in the far southeast of Sicily (province of Syracuse), some 250 km from the Palermo-area towns of the Covelli and Muffoletto lines — a distance that makes how their son ended up marrying into a Lascari family the standing puzzle of this branch.

He surfaces once more, twenty years on. On 22 May 1908 he walked into the study of the notary Paolo-Emilio Giardina on the Piano Vittorio Emanuele in Pachino and gave his formal consent to Salvatore's marriage to Carmela Covelli, a girl from Lascari he had probably never met. The act describes him as massaro and possidente, born and domiciled in Pachino, personally known to the notary — and, in the fixed phrase of Italian records, fu Domenico: son of a Domenico Barnisi already dead. That one word carries the line back a generation. Asked to sign, Giuseppe again could not, declaring that he had never learned; the witnesses signed for him. It is the last we see of him.

Two days earlier, on 20 May, the registrar of Pachino had drawn up the certificate of free status that the Lascari wedding required — confirming that no marriage for Salvatore stood on the Pachino index. In naming the boy's parents he wrote figlio di Giuseppe e di fu Barnizzi Carmela: son of Giuseppe, and of the late Carmela. Giuseppe's name carries no such word. Carmela was already dead when her son married, and Giuseppe went to the notary a widower.

Open question — a surname that does not exist. Barnisi has no bearers in Italy today. Neither do Barnesi, Barnizzi, Barnirzi or Barnizri — all return nothing against a database of some 180,000 Italian surnames covering over ninety per cent of households, which returns results normally for control names. Both Giuseppe and Carmela were illiterate, and neither could correct a clerk; every spelling we have was taken down by ear from people who could not write it. Whatever this family was called, we may not yet have seen it written correctly.
Open question — where are the others? A survey of about eighty Sicilian records containing every plausible spelling — Barnisi, Barnesi, Barnese, Barnizzi, Bargnesi, Bernesi, Barresi, Randisi — turned up living Barnese families in the provinces of Palermo (Castelbuono, Chiusa Sclafani, Pioppo, Mezzojuso, Valledolmo), Catania (the city, Palagonia), Enna (Villarosa), Trapani and Agrigento (Burgio). Not one in the province of Siracusa outside this family at Pachino. Page-by-page reading of the Pachino registers has likewise turned up no Barnisi at all — no cousins, no neighbours, nobody. A surname native to a town recurs in its registers; this one does not. Most apparent matches elsewhere dissolve on inspection. A Barresi Stefano of Lipari (b. ~1849), the standing marriage witness of that town, is indexed as "Barnese" in at least ten acts and signs Barresi in every one. A dozen more are only a registrar's or priest's signature at the foot of an act. The 1908 notarial act's own reading was tested against the alternative Randisi — a real Sicilian name, and one an Italian court formally substituted for "Barnisi" in a 1921 Naples death record — and rejected: the capital on image 2077 is unmistakably a B.
An untested possibility — a foundling ancestor. Two separate Sicilian institutions assigned Barnese as an invented surname to abandoned infants: the Modica brefotrofio in 1919, and the Catania foundling wheel, where a newborn was christened Teresa Barnese. Foundling homes worked from rotating lists of coined surnames. Set beside a name with no living bearers, a family with no traceable kin, and spelling that never settled, this raises the possibility that an ancestor entered the record as a foundling and was given the name. It cannot have been Giuseppe, who had a named father; it would have to be Domenico himself, or Domenico's father, born around 1800–1815, when the practice was routine. This is a hypothesis, not a finding — ordinary surnames also die out, and institutions sometimes borrowed real local names for their lists. It is testable: a foundling record says esposto or di genitori ignoti and names the institution.
A coincidence worth checking — Castelbuono. The standing puzzle of this branch is how a boy from Pachino came to be living at Via Umberto 38 in Lascari, 250 km away, and to marry a girl from there. The survey found an Angiola Barnese, daughter of the late Vincenzo and the late Antonia Marzola, at Castelbuono — roughly ten kilometres from Lascari, in the same stretch of the Madonie behind Cefalù. Nothing connects her to Giuseppe, and a north-coast family does not explain a southeastern one. But if Salvatore had kin near Cefalù, the improbable marriage stops being improbable. Castelbuono and Lascari sit in the same Palermo holdings as the records already gathered here, so the question is a narrow one: does the name appear in Lascari itself, or in Castelbuono, around 1908? ?
