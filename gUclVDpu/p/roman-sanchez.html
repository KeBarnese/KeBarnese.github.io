Sanchez · Guzman (Spain & Mexico)

Roman L. Sanchez

1892 – 1959
Great-grandfather
Older generations — On his parents: Roman's 1919 Los Angeles marriage license names Juan Sanchez and Agueda Lovato (spelled Lovato or Loveto) — though his own 1940 aliases argue the surname was really Lozano, see below. The 1916 Rochambeau manifest instead named a Domingo Sanchez as the relative back home, and named him for Andres Jose rather than for Roman — most likely a surviving uncle rather than the father, since Roman was said to have been orphaned young. Naming their 1925 daughter Agueda independently supports the mother's first name.

That field is the county of death, so whether he died in the city of Santa Clara, in San Jose, or elsewhere in the county is ? — the death certificate itself would say)
Married

On his 1940 registration he entered "none" for a wife but checked the box for widowed rather than divorced — but Ramona was living in 1940 and outlived him by seven years, dying in 1966, so the checkbox is either an error or his own way of calling the marriage finished)
Parents
Juan Sanchez & Agueda Lozano — first name from his 1919 marriage license (written there as Lovato / Loveto); surname corrected from the aliases he gave on his own 1940 alien registration (see note below)
Siblings

Children
John Claude (1920), James Ceferino (1922), Agueda (1925 – died in infancy), Roman 'Babe' Jr. (1926–2015), Cecelia (Morales) (1928), and Grace / Mary Grace (1932)
Occupation
Merchant seaman (his trade already on his 1916 manifest); railroad laborer (New York Central, N.Y.) in his twenties; then a painter for the rest of his working life — "painter, working for himself" (1920), "interior decorator" (1928), "painter, house" (1930), and by 1940 self-employed in South San Francisco at "Painting, Commercial Art" The trade never changed; the way he described it rose and fell. Interior decorator in 1928 is the high point, two years before the census found him owning a $4,000 house — and a long way from the sign-painting he was doing alone in 1940
Emigration
Two arrivals at New York in 1916. First as a passenger aboard the S.S. Rochambeau out of Bordeaux, arriving 20 Mar 1916, travelling with Manuel and Andres Jose — his occupation already entered on the manifest as "sailor." Then again on 18 Jun 1916 aboard the S.S. Glasgow, this time signed on as a crew member, admitted as a seaman (his 1940 alien registration). Whether the June entry was a return from a working voyage, or whether he mis-stated it in 1940 as his first arrival, is ?
Naturalization
Never completed. He received his first papers in April 1917 — by 1940 he could no longer recall whether at Indianapolis or Cincinnati — but never filed the petition that should have followed. Registered as an alien at South San Francisco on 19 Sep 1940 under the Alien Registration Act, Alien File A-2727457. On that form he gave his nationality as "none, last of Spain" — stateless after twenty-four years, a citizen of nowhere

Description

Court record
A house lost, then a marriage: on 1 Apr 1929 he and Ramona together took out a Deed of Trust with the Guarantee Building and Loan Association of San Bernardino, on Lot 29, Block 4, Arrowhead Park Unit No. 1 — presumably the Electric Ave. house recorded on the 1930 census. They defaulted; the successor lender, Pacific States Savings and Loan Company, recorded a notice of breach on 25 Jul 1932, and by Oct 1932 a trustee's sale was scheduled for 15 Dec 1932 at the San Bernardino courthouse steps No record has turned up of whether the sale went through or was averted. Either way it lands about eleven months before the Nov 1933 Pomona non-support case below found them already living apart — the lost house and the broken marriage sit right next to each other in time, though nothing here proves one caused the other

Two non-support proceedings, four years apart. A Pomona Progress-Bulletin item of 24 Nov 1933 reports "Roman L. Sanchez, 41, of Los Angeles, was to be tried before Judge U. E. White in justice court" on a misdemeanor charge of failure to provide for minor children — "his wife, the complaining witness, is a resident of Pomona." Separately, his own 1940 alien registration discloses an arrest on 20 Jan 1937, also at Pomona, for non-support, with a sentence of five months in a work camp. Whether these are two distinct charges, or one event that Roman misdated by three years when recalling it for the 1940 form, is ? — the 1933 clipping only reports that a trial was scheduled, not its outcome, so it may not have led to a conviction at all. Either way, it moves the documented start of the breakup back to at least 1933: by then Ramona was already living apart from him in Pomona and had brought the complaint herself
Grave marker
? — with the 1959 death now fixed to Santa Clara County, a burial there is the place to look

Their story

A fisherman's son from a tiny village in the Asturian mountains, he came to America twice in the same year. In March 1916 he stepped off the Rochambeau at New York, a passenger out of Bordeaux, nineteen by the manifest's reckoning and already calling himself a sailor. Three months later he came through again — this time not as a passenger at all but as crew, signed aboard a vessel he remembered as the Glasgow and admitted as a seaman. That detail explains a great deal: he was a working mariner before he was an immigrant, and the sea, not a steamship ticket, was how he got here.

His first years ashore were spent not in California but in upstate New York, working the railroads: his 1917 draft card finds him single and unemployed in Lackawanna, and by September 1918 he was in Solvay, near Syracuse, a laborer for the New York Central Railroad — still a Spanish subject, naming his father Juan Sanchez back home as next of kin. Somewhere in that stretch, in April 1917, he took out first citizenship papers in the Ohio valley, and then let them lapse. He never finished. Twenty-four years later, filling out his alien registration in South San Francisco, he wrote his nationality as none, last of Spain — he had let go of one country without ever taking hold of another.

Only afterward did he head west, where he worked as a fisherman and a painter, married Ramona in 1920, and raised a family that did not hold. There was a house, for a while: in April 1929 he and Ramona together took out a loan on a property in San Bernardino's Arrowhead Park tract, the Electric Ave. house the 1930 census would later find them owning. It didn't last — they defaulted, and by the fall of 1932 the lender had scheduled a trustee's sale to take it back. The break came earlier than the family seems to have remembered it, and close on the heels of that lost house: a Pomona newspaper reported him set for trial on a non-support charge in November 1933, just eleven months after the foreclosure sale was set, with Ramona — by then living apart from him in Pomona — as the complaining witness. Whether that 1933 case is the same one he later described to a government clerk as a January 1937 arrest and five-month work-camp sentence, or a separate, earlier episode entirely, isn't settled. What is clear is that by the early 1930s the marriage was already over in practice, years before 1937 — the hard fact underneath his children's scattering, and the reason his son James spent those years in Mexico. By 1940 he was living alone at 308 2nd Lane in South San Francisco, self-employed, painting signs and commercial art: five foot six and a half, a hundred and eighty-five pounds, brown hair, brown eyes. He listed five children living in the United States and no wife. One brother is said to have gone to Argentina; family lore held that he and Ramona were both orphans.

He died on 13 February 1959, aged sixty-six, in Santa Clara County — thirty-odd miles down the peninsula from the South San Francisco room where the alien registration had found him nineteen years earlier. He had been in America forty-three years by then and had never become a citizen of it.

Open question — the two 1916 arrivals. The Rochambeau manifest and the AR-2 are not in conflict; they record different crossings. On 20 March 1916 Roman came into New York as a passenger from Bordeaux with Manuel and Andres Jose, already listing his trade as sailor. The AR-2 he swore in 1940 reports arriving 18 June 1916 aboard the S.S. Glasgow as a crew member, admitted as a seaman — three months later, and in a completely different capacity. The likeliest reading is that he shipped out that spring as working crew and re-entered in June, then remembered the June entry as the one that fixed his status. He did, however, also give 18 June 1916 as his first arrival, which the Rochambeau manifest contradicts. To resolve: the June 1916 entry would sit on a crew list, not a passenger list — a separate section filed at the back of each vessel's manifest — which is why name-indexed passenger searches have never turned it up.
Open question — were they brothers? On the Rochambeau sheet the three young men are written consecutively, but each names a different relative back in Ciaño: Manuel a mother, Roman a mother who appears to read Manuela, and Andres Jose a father, Domingo Sanchez — the same Domingo the older-generations note above tries to place. Full brothers should name the same parents. They may be cousins rather than brothers, as family tradition has it. The handwriting is faint at present resolution and should be re-read from the original image before anything here is treated as settled.
His mother's surname. The AR-2 records two aliases in Roman's own account: Roman Sanchez Lozano and Lozano Sanchez. Under Spanish naming custom the second surname is the mother's, which points to Lozano — not the "Lovato / Loveto" written on his 1919 Los Angeles marriage license. A clerk taking the name down by ear in Los Angeles is a far likelier source of error than Roman writing his own mother's name, so Agueda Lozano is probably the correct form.
